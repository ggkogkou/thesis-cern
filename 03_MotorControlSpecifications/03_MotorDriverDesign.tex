\chapter{Motor and Control Architecture Selection}
\label{chapter:motor-control-selection}

In this chapter, an attempt is made to provide a meaningful high-level comparison between the different types of motors that are considered for this application. However, instead of providing an in-depth introduction to the motors, this chapter focuses only on the trade-offs and rationale behind selecting the motor and the control algorithm.

As noted in the thesis scope, Section~\ref{section:thesis-objectives}, the motor is treated as a radiation-tolerant device.

\section{Motor Technology Selection}
\label{sec:motor-technology-selection}
Several electric motor technologies can be considered for low-voltage robotic and mechatronic systems. In this thesis, the comparison is limited to brushed DC motors, stepper motors, and Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSM). These motor types are commonly used in embedded motion-control applications and can be driven from low-voltage DC supplies.

\subsection{Brushed DC Motors}
\label{sec:brushed-dc-motors}
Brushed DC (BDC) motors are among the simplest electric motors to drive. Unlike the other types of motors that will be presented, their commutation is done mechanically, meaning that they do not necessarily require a controller to modulate the currents in the windings~\cite{microchip_an905}. This is achieved through a commutator, a segmented conductive ring mounted on the motor's shaft, against which spring-loaded carbon brushes are pressed. As the shaft rotates, the brushes make contact with successive commutator segments, each connected to a different rotor winding, which reverses the current direction in the windings at the appropriate moments to sustain rotation~\cite{microchip_an905}.

The effective voltage on the motor windings is typically adjusted using PWM signals, with the H-bridge circuit enabling bi-directional motion. When precise control is required, a closed loop is added, usually based on a PID controller with position or velocity feedback. This simplicity makes brushed DC motors attractive for low-cost applications where precise control, high efficiency, or long lifetime are not the main requirements.

However, brushed DC motors have several limitations. The brushes and commutator are subject to wear since they continuously slide against each other during operation, which reduces lifetime and increases maintenance requirements~\cite{microchip_an905}. Brush contact can also introduce electrical noise and reliability concerns. For applications where the motor is difficult to access, or where long operational lifetime is required, these limitations become significant~\cite{nasa_motor_selection}.

\subsection{Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSM)}
\label{sec:bldc-motors}
Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs) perform commutation electronically rather than mechanically and do not require brushes or a commutator, thereby reducing mechanical wear and improving thermal performance, efficiency, and lifetime~\cite{nasa_motor_selection}. The term PMSM is often used interchangeably with brushless DC (BLDC). In practice both are permanent-magnet synchronous machines, but the distinction typically lies in the shape of the Back Electromotive Force (Back-EMF): trapezoidal in the case of BLDC and sinusoidal in the case of PMSM.

Schematically, a PMSM/BLDC consists of windings placed in slots on the stator, and permanent magnets on the rotor. The stator windings are modulated by the driving electronics, creating a magnetic field $\boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{stator}}$. This field interacts with the magnetic field produced by the rotor's permanent magnets, $\boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{rotor}}$, generating mechanical torque
\begin{equation*}
    \boldsymbol{\vec{T}} \propto 
    \boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{rotor}} \times 
    \boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{stator}}
\end{equation*}
with maximum torque produced when the angle between the two fields is $\theta = 90^\circ$.

Another important concept is that of pole pairs. The simplified, single-pole-pair schematic commonly used for illustration is not representative of real machines, which typically contain multiple permanent magnets on the rotor and a correspondingly larger number of slots and windings on the stator. Figure~\ref{fig:bldc-mps} compares a one-pole-pair (two-pole) machine with a two-pole-pair (four-pole) machine; this construction can be scaled up to an arbitrary number of pole pairs. The number of pole pairs introduces a distinction between the mechanical angle of the rotor and its electrical angle, the latter being the relevant quantity for commutation and control; the relationship between the two, and its role in Field-Oriented Control, will be introduced in Section~\ref{sec:field-oriented-control}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/BLDC_MPS.JPG}
    \caption{Schematic representation of a BLDC motor with one and two pole pairs. Image taken from \cite{mps_dc_motors_article}.}
    \label{fig:bldc-mps}
\end{figure}

A BLDC motor requires a three-phase inverter circuit and a control algorithm to energize the motor phases according to rotor position. This position can either be measured using sensors such as Hall-effect sensors or encoders (sensored commutation), or estimated from the motor's physical properties using dedicated estimators (sensorless commutation).

\subsection{Stepper Motors}
\label{sec:stepper-motors}

Stepper motors are electric motors designed to produce discrete angular movements, referred to as steps, and thereby reach a desired position precisely. Depending on their construction, the rotor may contain permanent magnets, ferromagnetic teeth, or a combination of both. Most stepper motors have two stator phases, although three- and five-phase designs also exist; these phases are energized cyclically, producing magnetic fields with which the rotor tends to align and thereby generating torque~\cite{faulhaber_stepper_basics}. These phases can be driven in full-step, half-step, or microstepping mode, the latter subdividing each full step into smaller increments to improve smoothness and resolution at the cost of reduced incremental torque~\cite{faulhaber_stepper_basics,faulhaber_microstepping}.

Stepper motors are widely used in low-cost positioning systems because they can provide accurate and repeatable positioning in open-loop operation, without requiring a position sensor under normal operating conditions. Their main advantages include simple control, good low-speed torque, and the ability to hold their position while energized, known as holding torque.

However, an open-loop stepper motor may lose steps when the applied load exceeds the available torque~\cite{faulhaber_step_loss_tutorial}. Its torque also decreases at higher speeds because winding inductance and back-EMF limit the phase current~\cite{faulhaber_stepper_basics}. Stepper motors are therefore commonly driven from a voltage higher than their rated winding voltage, while the current is actively limited to prevent overheating.

\section{Robotics and Positioning Systems at CERN}
\label{sec:state-of-the-art-application-context}

\subsubsection*{MIRA (Measurement \& Inspection Robot for Accelerators)}
MIRA is a robotic platform designed to perform radiation surveys in the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) \cite{forkel2022telerobotic,forkel2025mira}. Its omnidirectional mobile base comprises four parallel Mecanum wheels, enabling movement in any direction — including sideways and diagonally — as well as rotation about its own axis \cite{prados2021omnidirectional,forkel2025mira}. The platform has a compact design that allows it to pass through the small cutout gaps in the doors separating the various SPS sectors \cite{prados2021omnidirectional,forkel2022telerobotic}.

The base integrates 24~V, 20~W Maxon RE 25 brushed DC motors with graphite brushes, each paired with a Maxon GP 26A planetary gearhead with a reduction ratio of 27:1 \cite{prados2021omnidirectional}. Wheel position and speed are measured by ENC 16 Easy Absolute XT encoders from the maxon group \cite{forkel2025mira}. A 6-DoF Kinova Gen2 robotic arm is mounted on top of the platform, integrating Maxon EC 45 brushless DC motors as part of its servomechanisms \cite{maxon2015jaco2}, with a radiation sensor attached to its end effector \cite{forkel2022telerobotic,forkel2025mira}.

\subsubsection*{CRANEbot}
CRANEbot is a teleoperated robot raised by overhead cranes, allowing it to perform tasks from height~\cite{dago2024cranebotteleoperated}. It is able to carry out interventions in radioactive areas not reachable by common terrestrial robots, and thus replace humans who would otherwise risk working in a hazardous environment. As it can carry payloads of up to 300~kg, it is used for remote transport and manipulation of dangerous equipment, as well as investigation of unknown objects~\cite{dago2024cranebotteleoperated, dago2024modelling}. Precise handling is made possible by the integrated 6-DoF SCHUNK LWA~4P robotic arms~\cite{schunklwa4p} or the Pilz PRBT6~\cite{pilzprbt6}, both of which are compatible with the platform.

\subsubsection*{CERNbot}
Similarly to the CRANEbot, the CERNbot \cite{DiCastro2017CERNbot} is a modular battery-powered teleoperated vehicle that can host one or two SCHUNK robotic arms and carries out procedures remotely with the required degree of precision \cite{Gentile2023}. Its primary uses are remote manipulation and disposal of dangerous equipment, handling of tooling, and inspection of rooms \cite{DiCastro2017CERNbot, Gentile2023}. The base of the robotic platform integrates maxon BLDC motors \cite{Matheson2023, DiCastro2017CERNbot}.

The \textit{SCHUNK LWA~4P} manipulator operates from a 24~V DC supply and integrates BLDC motors with permanent magnetic brakes and absolute position sensing as part of its servo-mechanism~\cite{schunk_lwa4p_datasheet}. The \textit{Pilz PRBT6} is a 24~V DC servo-driven manipulator with brushless servomotors, spring-force brakes, and absolute magnetic encoders~\cite{Pilz2018PRBT, Matheson2023}.

\section{Motor Type Selection}
\label{sec:motor-type-selection}
Brushed DC motors are excluded from this comparison, as qualifying brush-based motors for long-life or harsh-environment applications is both costly and time-consuming, and brushless technology has been shown to offer superior life, torque, and efficiency for such applications~\cite{nasa_motor_selection, acdcfan2024brushless}. Modern robotic arms and servo drives predominantly rely on either stepper motors or PMSMs.

Stepper motors are popular for their inherent positioning precision, owed to their high pole-pair count and holding torque. However, in a radiation environment the digital control unit will suffer from SEE, while all components degrade through cumulative effects such as TID and TNID, compromising controllability over time. A closed-loop system is therefore essential to meet the safety standards required of a motor driving system, as it compensates for long-term drift, provides diagnostics and fault detection, and maximizes observability of the system's behavior.

While any motor type can be operated in closed loop, the PMSM (or BLDC) is the most "generic" choice, performing torque, velocity, or position control equally well, which makes this prototype a suitable reference for future applications beyond robotic-arm positioning. As discussed in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:selected-approach}, many of the control methods developed for PMSMs extend naturally to other motor types. Furthermore, qualifying a PMSM control system is the most demanding case, so the irradiation testing performed here will surface the broadest range of potential vulnerabilities, providing a solid reference for future, more sophisticated designs.

\section{Control Methods for BLDC/PMSM Motors}
\label{sec:bldc-control-methods}

BLDC motors can be controlled using different methods, depending on the required performance, available sensors, computational resources, and implementation complexity. The most common approaches include trapezoidal control, sinusoidal control, and field-oriented control.

\subsection{Trapezoidal Control}
\label{sec:trapezoidal-control}

Trapezoidal control, also known as six-step commutation, is one of the simplest methods to drive BLDC motors. In this method, two motor phases are energized at a time, while the third phase is left Hi-Z. The commutation sequence is determined from the rotor position, which can be measured using Hall sensors or estimated from the Back-EMF.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/Trapezoidal_Control.pdf}
  \caption{Trapezoidal control}
  \label{fig:trap-control}
\end{figure}

It is important to intuitively understand how it works and what is its disadvantage. In figure \ref{fig:trap-control}, the blue arrow represents the magnetic field vector of the stator and the red one represents the rotor's. Supposing a CW rotation, and since only two phases are driven at each time, the electrical angle is advancing $60^\circ$ at a time and the rotor's magnetic field is "chasing" the stator's in order to end up aligned (that's from electromagnetics theory). So, the rotor does not stay at the $90^\circ$ which is the maximum torque producing but is rotating in the yellow area till the next windings pair is driven.

The main advantage of trapezoidal control is its simplicity. It requires relatively low computational resources and is suitable for applications where moderate torque ripple and acoustic noise are acceptable. On the negatives, it is expected to produce torque ripple, vibration, and reduced performance at low speed. That makes it suitable for applications like fans, or whatever.

\subsection{Sinusoidal Control}
\label{sec:sinusoidal-control}

Sinusoidal control improves upon trapezoidal control by applying sinusoidal phase currents to the motor windings. This reduces torque ripple and can improve smoothness of operation. However, sinusoidal control requires more accurate rotor position information and more complex current regulation compared with six-step commutation.

The idea is to modulate PWM signal in order to control the effective voltage on the motor phases. Since motor phases are inductive elements the current resists the change and thus it acts as an RL filter that smooths out the wave signal and the current is sinusoidal.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/Sinusoidal_PWM_Diagram.pdf}
  \caption{Sinusoidal PWM and the current that the motor sees in its windings}
  \label{fig:sinusoidal-pwm}
\end{figure}

Although sinusoidal control can provide smoother operation, it does not fully decouple the torque-producing and flux-producing components of the stator current. For applications requiring high-performance torque control, field-oriented control is generally preferred.

\subsection{Comparison of Trapezoidal and Sinusoidal}
Theoretically, trapezoidal control is the de facto commutation method for BLDC motors, as the back-EMF shape is trapezoidal. On the other hand, for the PMSM, which is AC and whose back-EMF shape is sinusoidal, the best commutation method is sinusoidal. In reality, rarely is the shape of any motor perfectly trapezoidal, but mostly they tend to resemble sinusoids. The way to determine whether a motor is trapezoidal or sinusoidal is to operate it in generator mode, meaning to connect resistors in a star or delta shape on the motor phases and spin the rotor with an electric drill. With an oscilloscope, it will be visible whether the phase-to-phase voltages are sinusoidal or trapezoidal.

\subsection{Field-Oriented Control}
\label{sec:field-oriented-control}

Field-oriented control, also known as vector control, transforms the three-phase stator currents into a rotating reference frame aligned with the rotor magnetic field. This allows the motor currents to be separated into two components: one associated with the magnetic flux and one associated with torque production. As a result, the BLDC motor can be controlled in a manner similar to a separately excited DC motor.

The main advantage of field-oriented control is its ability to provide smooth and efficient torque control over a wide operating range. This makes it suitable for servo applications where accurate current, velocity, or position control is required. The disadvantage is the increased computational complexity and the need for accurate current measurement, rotor position information, and coordinate transformations.

For this thesis, field-oriented control is considered the most relevant control method because it enables high-performance closed-loop operation of BLDC motors. The following sections introduce the mathematical transformations and control structure used for vector control.

\subsubsection{Clarke \& Park Transformations}

The Clarke and Park transformations, as well as their inverse, are another very important aspect of FOC. The core idea of FOC is to move from the three-dimensional stationary abc-frame to the rotational dq-frame. In order to provide some context on the transformations, the mathematical definition accompanied by the application to motor phases will be shown.

\textbf{Clarke Transformation} \\
In a 3-phase sinusoidal system, the currents and voltages are typically $120^\circ$ apart. This means that there is redundant "information" in the voltage $\textbf{V} = \left[V_a, V_b, V_c\right]$ and current $\textbf{I} = \left[I_a, I_b, I_c\right]$ vectors. 

The idea behind this mathematical operation (that was modeled by Edith Clarke) is to simplify the three-dimensional reference frame that is used in three-phase inverters (the so called abc-frame) and project it into an equivalent two-dimensional stationary frame, usually mentioned as $\alpha\beta$-frame. That means to reduce the system complexity by removing the redundant information. Mathematically, the transformation is expressed as

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_\alpha \\ u_\beta \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_a \\ u_b \\ u_c \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:clarke-matrix}
\end{equation}

Finally, the most important takeaway is to intuitively understand that both the $120^\circ$ abc-frame and the derived $\alpha\beta$-frame are stationary, and that means that the frame remains fixed to the stator. So, informally, we can say that the calculations happen from the "stators'" point of view. The Clarke transformation is used together with the Park transformation to transform a 3-phase motor model into a dq-frame.

\textbf{Electrical Angle} \\
Referring back to Subsection~\ref{sec:bldc-motors} and Figure~\ref{fig:bldc-mps}, PMSM/BLDC motors can have different numbers of pole pairs depending on their construction. This can be counterintuitive when compared to the diagrams that showcase the vectors $\boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{rotor}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\vec{B}}_{\text{stator}}$, since those vectors are derived from a one-pole-pair representation.

In practice, throughout the transformation to the dq-frame and the SVPWM calculation, it is not the mechanical angle taken directly from the position sensor that is used. The mechanical angle $\theta_m$ represents the actual rotor position in a motor with multiple pole pairs; it is therefore transformed into the equivalent electrical angle $\theta_e$, which represents the rotor position in an equivalent one-pole-pair system. This mapping is given by Equation~\ref{eq:electrical-angle}.

\begin{equation}
    \theta_e [^\circ] = N_{\text{pole-pairs}} \times \theta_m [^\circ]
    \label{eq:electrical-angle}
\end{equation}

The algorithm therefore always uses the electrical angle internally in FOC; all mechanical angles are transformed into their corresponding electrical angles before being consumed by the controller.

\textbf{Park Transformation} \\
These transformations unlock one of the main advantages of field-oriented control (FOC): the ability to decouple magnetic flux control from torque control. In FOC, the reference frame is chosen such that the flux-producing and torque-producing components can be controlled independently. The components of the $\alpha\beta$-frame and the electrical angle $\theta_e$ are the required inputs. The transformation is mathematically expressed as

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_d \\ u_q \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_e) & \sin(\theta_e) \\ -\sin(\theta_e) & \cos(\theta_e) \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_\alpha \\ u_\beta \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:park-matrix}
\end{equation}

with the inverse of it being

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_\alpha \\ u_\beta \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_e) & -\sin(\theta_e) \\ \sin(\theta_e) & \cos(\theta_e) \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} u_d \\ u_q \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:park-inverse-matrix}
\end{equation}

The decoupling is accomplished by the Park transformation that transforms the stationary $\alpha\beta$-frame into a rotating dq-frame, where d stands for direct and q for quadrature. For BLDC/PMSM this frame is typically fixed to the rotor meaning that intuitively the calculations happen from the rotor's point of view. This can be visualized by thinking that we don't modulate the stator anymore but directly the rotor and its axis d and q.

\subsubsection{The PID Controller}

The PID controller is another core component of FOC. Since it is well known to most engineers, it will only be briefly presented. If $r(t)$ is the reference signal and $\phi$ is the controlled variable, the classical PID controller in the time domain is written as
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = K_P \cdot e(t) + K_I \int_0^t e(t)\,dt + K_D \frac{d}{dt}\left\{ e(t) \right\}
\end{equation}
In FOC, it is standard practice to use a PI controller for the inner-loop d- and q-axis currents~\cite{busarello2025comprehensive}.

\subsubsection{Space-Vector Modulation PWM}
Space-Vector PWM (SVPWM) waveform generation is also one of the core blocks of FOC. Essentially, it is a modulation technique that generates the PWM signals which drive the inverter. SVPWM's main advantage is the generation of sinusoidal-like output voltages, and the technique is also used more broadly in DC/AC converters. Its key advantage over traditional Sinusoidal PWM (SPWM) is that it increases DC bus voltage utilization by approximately 15\%~\cite{vanderbroeck1988pwm}, achieved by injecting a third-order harmonic (or higher odd multiples, such as the ninth, fifteenth, etc.) into each phase reference. This raises the maximum achievable fundamental phase-to-phase voltage without exceeding the inverter's voltage limits, and thus increases the available power of the motor for a given DC bus voltage.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/spwm_waveforms.pdf}
    \caption{Phase-to-ground reference waveforms for SPWM.}
    \label{fig:spwm-waveforms}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/svpwm_waveforms.pdf}
    \caption{Phase-to-ground reference waveforms for SVPWM with third-harmonic injection.}
    \label{fig:svpwm-waveforms}
\end{figure}

Figures~\ref{fig:spwm-waveforms} and~\ref{fig:svpwm-waveforms} present the SPWM and SVPWM phase-to-ground reference waveforms, respectively. The apparent ``distortion'' in the SVPWM signal is the artificially added third-order harmonic component. The key point is that the motor does not respond to the phase-to-ground voltage, but to the line-to-line voltage, which is the difference between two phase references. When this subtraction is performed, the third-order harmonic cancels exactly, since it is identical in all three phases~\cite{microchip2024zsm}. The resulting line-to-line fundamental amplitude is nevertheless higher, which is the source of the approximately 15\% improvement in DC bus utilization, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:spwm-svpwm-comparison}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/line_to_line_voltage.pdf}
    \caption{Line-to-line voltage $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$ for SPWM and SVPWM,
             illustrating the improvement in achievable voltage swing with
             third-harmonic injection.}
    \label{fig:spwm-svpwm-comparison}
\end{figure}

In this coordinate system, every vector --- whether voltage or current --- can be expressed as a superposition of the base vectors $\vec{u}_x$, with $x \in \{a, b, c\}$. The hexagon is divided into six sectors, each bounded by two adjacent active switching states of the three-phase bridge, and the modulator synthesizes the reference voltage vector within each sector by computing the appropriate dwell times for those states~\cite{vanderbroeck1988pwm}.

While what was just described is the Conventional SVPWM (CSVPWM) technique, there are alternatives that instead perform Zero-Sequence Modulation (ZSM): rather than computing sector angles and dwell times, they apply a different zero-sequence (common-mode) offset directly to the unshifted per-phase duty cycles, which requires far less computation. For this thesis, given the limited computational budget of the target microcontroller, this computational simplicity was the deciding factor in the choice of technique.

Therefore, in the context of this thesis, the midpoint clamp method was implemented. Given the unshifted per-phase duty cycles $D_{a0}, D_{b0}, D_{c0}$ obtained from the Clarke transform, the zero-sequence offset is computed as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta D = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\max(D_{a0}, D_{b0}, D_{c0}) + \min(D_{a0}, D_{b0}, D_{c0})}{2}
    \label{eq:midpoint-clamp}
\end{equation}

and the final, duty cycles applied to the PWM peripheral are obtained by adding this offset:

\begin{align}
    D_a &= D_{a0} + \Delta D \\
    D_b &= D_{b0} + \Delta D \\
    D_c &= D_{c0} + \Delta D
\end{align}

This effectively centers the midpoint between the largest and smallest of the three duty cycles at $50\%$, which is mathematically equivalent to identifying the SVPWM sector and computing dwell times directly~\cite{microchip2024zsm}, but without requiring trigonometric functions or explicit sector identification.

\subsubsection{Startup System Calibration}
One of the fundamental procedures before FOC starts is the startup calibration of the system. This procedure includes the determination of the zero-offset electrical angle and the determination of the rotation direction.

\textbf{Zero-offset angle} calibration is necessary because at startup the angle that the position encoder, either incremental or absolute, translates as $0^\circ$ is not necessarily aligned with what the actual rotor \textit{"sees"} as $0^\circ$, namely the d-axis. One of the solutions for that is to apply a strong current to the d-axis for a short but sufficient amount of time and let the rotor lock onto that position~\cite{ti2021instaspin}. At this point, the offset between the d-axis and the encoder zero reference is measurable. This must be stored and subtracted from the encoder measurements at each iteration of the algorithm.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{03_MotorControlSpecifications/images/ZeroOffsetElAngle.pdf}
  \caption{Demonstration of the zero angle offset calibration procedure.}
  \label{fig:zero-offset-calib}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Direction} calibration, essentially, refers to the connection of the phases to the motor driver. If phases are disconnected and reconnected in a different pattern, the same modulation might lead to commutation in the opposite direction. Multiple solutions exist in the literature to address this, one of the simplest among them being to move the rotor in open-loop commutation for a short interval and then compare the final position to the starting one. The trade-off in this method is that if the load is too much for the torque applied with the open-loop, the rotor will stall~\cite{ti2021instaspin}.

\section{Selected Approach}
\label{sec:selected-approach}
Among the control algorithms discussed, Field-Oriented Control (FOC) was selected. It is well suited to servo and robotic arm applications, generally offering the best behavior under dynamic loading, and is versatile enough to support torque, velocity, and position control modes across many motor types, including DC and AC PMSMs, AC induction machines (ACIMs), and stepper motors. This versatility, combined with the additional control options it enables, has made FOC (or its derivatives) the industry standard for PMSM motor control.

As established in Section~\ref{sec:motor-type-selection}, a closed-loop algorithm is necessary in high-radiation environments, where TID and displacement damage progressively degrade the electronics. A feedback loop can compensate for some of these effects, such as current leakage, and is also the only way to detect failures caused by SEE, which would otherwise go unnoticed in an open-loop system.

FOC offers two further advantages specific to the robots discussed earlier. First, since torque and flux control are mathematically decoupled, all current is guaranteed to be torque-pro\-ducing, eliminating wasted power and maximizing torque per watt, which is beneficial for battery-operated robotic platforms such as MIRA. Second, FOC provides accurate torque control with minimal ripple even at low speeds, which is essential for precise robotic manipulation tasks.

Even if FOC is not the final approach adopted, qualifying a system of this complexity under radiation still provides confidence in the feasibility of simpler systems with reduced control complexity. Separately, FOC is also under investigation as a way to mitigate the noise introduced by the 1~km cable runs in the LHC collimator stepper motor system.

Finally, FOC is being investigated for collimator stepper motors as a way to improve dynamic response and reduce vibrations during motion, while additional standstill techniques may be needed to avoid residual motor hunting~\cite{merino2019vibration}.
