\chapter{System-Level Radiation Tests and Results}
\label{chapter:rad-tests-results}

This chapter presents and analyzes the system-level irradiation tests conducted at the CHARM and CC60 facilities. Rather than presenting all test runs, the focus is placed selectively on those that yielded important observations.

All discussed tests utilized a similar positioning test sequence, where four different angles were commanded cyclically and sequentially to evaluate performance under irradiation.

\section{\texorpdfstring{$^{60}$Co}{60Co} Irradiation Tests}
\label{sec:cc60-revised}

\subsection{L6498D Gate Drivers}
\label{subsection:l6498d-cc60-results}

The irradiation test was carried out at the CC60 facility using a Cobalt-60 source. For the purposes of the test, the motor setup was mounted on a frame in front of the radiation source, placed on a movable table at a specific distance that calibrates the dose rate at \SI{30}{Gy/h}.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cc60-revised}, the PCB was irradiated from the bottom side of the setup. This orientation was selected so that the aluminium structure and the motor would not shield part of the board from the incoming $\gamma$-ray irradiation.

The system was irradiated up to \SI{500}{Gy} and showcased two distinct failure modes: \textit{i) complete motion breakdown due to the failure of the L6498D gate drivers}, and \textit{ii) external watchdog timer (WDT) breakdown}. These failure modes were determined through a combination of resolver measurements and current consumption logs derived from the power supply unit (PSU).

  \caption{The test setup mounted on the moving table of CC60 facility, with the metallic structure facing outwards to avoid accidental shielding of one fraction of the DUT.}
  \label{fig:cc60-revised}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Failures Identification}

Firstly, the permanent loss of control is very easy to identify from the resolver's position data. In that regard, Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-resolver} serves a dual purpose. Firstly, it is used to identify the moment where the control is visibly lost, and secondly, visualize the theory behind the classification of position samples, onto settled and transient, that was developed during Subsection~\ref{subsection:position-samples-characterization} of Chapter~\ref{chapter:test-bench-design}.

More specifically, the shaded areas represent the samples that their velocity is below the threshold for them to be considered as settled. Furthermore, in the same figure, the permanent failure of the system is identified at approximately \SI{312.56}{Gy}; however, taking into account the currents from the motor power supply (see Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-20v}), the first jitter appeared at \SI{312.4}{Gy}, which is set as the actual failing point.

Monitoring the system's voltage rails proved highly effective for isolating and identifying these failures. Regarding the first, more straightforward failure, Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-5v} illustrates the current consumption of the \SI{5}{V} rail supplying the MCU. A sudden, sharp drop is immediately apparent, falling from a consistent mean of \SIrange{23}{24}{\milli\ampere} down to approximately \SIrange{11}{12}{\milli\ampere}. This behavior is attributed to a permanent failure of the external WDT (TPL5010); upon failing, it latched the active-low $\overline{\text{RST}}$ pin of the MCU to a low state, thereby forcing the MCU into a continuous reset, non-operational state. This specific failure was observed at an accumulated dose of approximately \SI{414.66}{Gy}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{06_RadiationTestsResults/images-l6498d/current_5v_rail.pdf}
    \caption{Current consumption of the \SI{5}{V} rail, showing the distinct drop in current caused by the permanent failure of the external WDT.}
    \label{fig:l6498d-5v}
\end{figure}

Following this, Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-12v} depicts the current consumption of the \SI{12}{V} power rail. As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:pcb-iter-2}, this rail supplies power to the three L6498D gate drivers and a single voltage reference. The filtered trend (represented by the blue moving average line) shows a significant, steady rise in current consumption up to a critical point. Beyond this threshold, as evidenced by the gray raw measurements, the rail experiences severe, erratic current spikes. This behavior directly correlates with the progressive degradation of the gate drivers, culminating in the complete breakdown of the motor control stage. After the failure point, the current continues to increase to the end of the full irradiation cycle, where it showcased an increase of \SI{740}{\percent}.

    \caption[Current consumption of the \SI{12}{V} rail during irradiation.]{Current consumption of the \SI{12}{V} rail, showing a rising moving average (blue) and erratic transient spikes (gray) prior to gate driver failure.}
    \label{fig:l6498d-12v}
\end{figure}

As an interesting point of comparison, Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-20v} provides an insight into the current spikes experienced on the \SI{20}{V} motor power rail. Because a moving average filter would mask these rapid transients, the blue line represents the raw, unfiltered measurements. Severe current spikes are observed, some of which reach the overcurrent limit of the PSU. This behavior provides a direct indication of shoot-through events occurring in the half-bridges of the inverter. Notably, the period where these spikes on the motor supply rail happen matches the period of the gate driver spikes perfectly.

    \label{fig:l6498d-20v}
\end{figure}

Therefore, for further analysis of TID effects on the control performance, the \SI{312.54}{Gy} threshold is taken as the ceiling, after which the control is considered lost and the system permanently damaged. Moreover, after inspection of the irradiated system, it appeared that all three gate drivers were damaged.

\subsubsection*{Repeatability During Irradiation}

The data post-processing and analysis are performed as discussed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:test-bench-design} and Subsection~\ref{subsection:data-post-processing}. For this specific test, the repeatability based on the resolver samples is evaluated. Further insights into the system performance are provided for the successful test run, which is described in Subsection~\ref{subsection:ncp5183-cc60-results}. Regarding the visualization of repeatability, dose windows of \SI{20}{\gray} were utilized with a step of \SI{50}{\gray}.

In Figure~\ref{fig:l6498d-repeatability-1-2}, the violin plots of the evaluated samples are presented. The evaluation of the repeatability is performed locally at every \SI{50}{\gray}, and a global representation is ultimately created that incorporates all the positions across the entire irradiation run. Violin plots are highly useful in this case since the distribution of the samples can be observed while still retaining the internal markers that provide the mean and standard deviation. As can be seen, the mean of the settled angles remains stable during the irradiation and is very close to the global mean. For instance, for Target Angle 1, the global mean is established at \SI{6.336}{\degree}, with the local window means drifting by less than \SI{0.05}{\degree} throughout the test. Furthermore, the shape of the distribution does not change significantly from a visual standpoint, which indicates that the repeatability remains consistent as the irradiation proceeds. Up to the point of failure, there is no drift in the mean, and the global repeatability distribution shows a very high concentration of samples toward the center.

\subsection{NCP5183 Gate Drivers}
\label{subsection:ncp5183-cc60-results}

This test was performed two days after the previously described irradiation test and followed an identical procedure. The only difference was the replacement of the L6498D gate drivers with the NCP5183 ones.

As a result, the setup remained fully functional up to an accumulated TID of approximately \SI{650}{\gray}, thus exceeding the initial TID target of \SI{500}{\gray} established in the thesis objectives (see Section~\ref{section:thesis-objectives} on page~\pageref{target:500gy-goal}).

\subsubsection*{Failure Point Identification}

Similarly to the process followed in Subsection~\ref{subsection:l6498d-cc60-results}, the failure point is derived from the resolver samples. In Figure~\ref{fig:ncp5183-resolver}, the system is observed to enter its failure state at approximately \SI{646.8}{\gray}. Past this point, erratic rotor movement is exhibited, characterized by significant noise, jitter, and vibration, before a complete failure mode is ultimately entered.

\subsection*{Conclusions}
\label{subsec:cc60-conclusions}

A comparison between Figure~\ref{fig:ncp5183-enc-3s} and Figure~\ref{fig:ncp5183-3s} shows that they are almost identical, indicating that the encoder exhibits deterministic behavior. Neither the L6498D nor the NCP5183 test revealed any measurable degradation in positioning repeatability attributable to TID effects. In both cases, the system maintained consistent repeatability across all target positions throughout the entire irradiation run, up to the respective points of failure.

The observed position-dependent variability in repeatability is more likely related to encoder--magnet misalignment or an excessive distance between the magnet and the encoder. Due to a manufacturing error during the CNC machining of the test bench, the magnet was positioned slightly farther from the sensor than originally designed. Given this mechanical misalignment, the results are considered consistent and do not indicate any radiation-induced degradation.

Moreover, since the system runs FOC, it relies heavily on PI controllers: two for the $dq$ currents, one for the velocity, and one proportional controller for the position. These controllers were not optimally tuned, and thus the system is expected to exhibit limited positioning performance independently of radiation effects. Therefore, it can be safely concluded that the system's performance is limited primarily by mechanical and control factors rather than radiation-induced degradation.

\subsection{First Radiation Test}
\label{subsection:charm-1}

The first trial was placed in the overhead position at CHARM for one full irradiation week, after which it suffered a mechanical failure rather than an electrical one. As a result, this test is not considered for the assessment of TID or displacement damage degradation. It is, however, analyzed for the MCU SEEs it produced, as it serves as a reference point for the SEE cross-sections against which the fault mitigation techniques implemented in the second test can be evaluated.

\subsection{Second Radiation Test}
\label{subsection:charm-2}

The second CHARM test campaign placed the same system in the overhead position for two irradiation weeks. This test provided significantly more insight into both the SEE behavior and the component-level degradation of the system.

Permanent failure of the system was identified at a TID of \SI{234.7}{\gray}. Post-irradiation troubleshooting confirmed that all three L6498D gate drivers had failed and were no longer outputting PWM signals despite receiving them at their inputs. This finding is consistent with the $^{60}$Co results of Subsection~\ref{subsection:l6498d-cc60-results}, where the same components were identified as the weakest point of the system. Notably, the TID threshold at which failure occurred was significantly lower than in the $^{60}$Co test, which isolates pure TID effects. This reduction is attributed to the contribution of high-energy hadrons in the mixed-field environment, which appear to have accelerated device degradation through displacement damage.

At approximately \SI{97}{\gray}, the USART peripheral responsible for telemetry logging was permanently damaged and did not recover. The USART was operating at \SI{460800}{bps}, transmitting 44-byte payloads at approximately 10 frames per second. Since the software watchdog used USART activity as the primary indicator of MCU SEFI, cross-section calculations for both Control Corruption and MCU SEFI events are considered valid only up to this point. Beyond it, erroneous resolver motion served as the fallback reference for the software watchdog. It is also noted that this test included the software triple modular redundancy (TMR) as a fault mitigation technique.

During the second CHARM irradiation campaign, a total of 11 Control Corruption events and 28 MCU SEFI events were recorded up to the loss of UART telemetry, at a total accumulated fluence of \SI{3.05e11}{\per\cm\squared}~HEH, a TID of \SI{108.6}{\gray}, and a displacement damage dose of \SI{1.55e12}{\per\cm\squared} (1\,MeV\,$n_\text{eq}$). The resulting cross-sections and their 95\% confidence level Poisson upper limits are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cross-sections-charm-2}.

\subsection{Conclusions of CHARM Tests}
\label{subsection:charm-conclusions}

The first test, while inconclusive with respect to TID and displacement damage degradation due to mechanical failure, established a baseline SEE cross-section for the system operating without fault mitigation. The second test reached end of life with the L6498D gate drivers confirmed as the failing components, consistent across both the CHARM and $^{60}$Co campaigns.

From an SEE perspective, the MCU SEFI cross-sections of the two tests are in close agreement, as expected for a process governed by the device's intrinsic sensitivity rather than the firmware. Specifically, the observed $\sigma_\text{SEFI}$ of Campaign~2 falls within the 95\% confidence level upper limit of Campaign~1, and vice versa, indicating that the two estimates are statistically compatible and consistent with a single underlying MCU cross-section. The Control Corruption cross-section was approximately 47\% lower in the second test, where the software TMR fault mitigation technique was active; however, the 95\% Poisson confidence intervals of the two campaigns overlap, so this difference does not reach statistical significance given the limited number of observed events ($N=21$ and $N=11$, respectively). The effectiveness of the implemented TMR mitigation in preventing radiation-induced errors from propagating into loss of motor controllability therefore remains inconclusive on the basis of the present dataset, and the measured cross-sections should instead be regarded as preliminary reference values for future, higher-statistics mitigation studies.

\section{Chapter Summary and Discussion}
\label{section:rad-tests-discussion}

Across all irradiation campaigns, neither the $^{60}$Co nor the CHARM tests revealed any measurable degradation in positioning repeatability attributable to radiation effects. The system's performance limitations throughout all tests were consistently traceable to mechanical factors --- primarily the encoder--magnet misalignment introduced by a manufacturing tolerance error in the test bench --- and to the suboptimal tuning of the FOC PI controllers, rather than to TID or displacement damage.

The gate drivers emerged as the critical limiting components in both radiation environments. The NCP5183 gate drivers demonstrated significantly superior TID tolerance compared to the L6498D under pure $^{60}$Co irradiation, comfortably exceeding the thesis TID target. Under the mixed-field environment at CHARM, the L6498D gate drivers failed at a TID significantly lower than that observed under pure $^{60}$Co irradiation. This reduction strongly suggests a synergistic contribution of displacement damage to the degradation of the L6498D. As explicitly stated in its datasheet, the L6498D relies on bipolar technology in its internal stages, which is known to be sensitive to displacement damage effects. The NCP5183, by contrast, does not reference bipolar technology in its datasheet, and its significantly superior TID tolerance under $^{60}$Co irradiation suggests a more radiation-hard internal architecture.

The DD/TID ratio measured at the CHARM test position was consistent across both irradiation weeks, confirming the stability of the mixed-field composition at the test position throughout the campaign. Given that the NCP5183 exceeded the TID target with a considerable margin under pure TID conditions, it is reasonable to expect that even with the additional degradation contribution from displacement damage in a mixed-field environment, the system would remain operational beyond the target. The evaluation of the NCP5183 under mixed-field irradiation conditions is, therefore, the natural next step toward a full radiation qualification of the system.
