\chapter{Experimental Setup: Design, Integration, and Evaluation Metrics}

This chapter describes the design of the complete system test setup developed for radiation testing of the motor controller. The objective was to build an easily reproducible test bench that minimises the overhead involved in replacing irradiated parts, updating firmware, or reconfiguring the data acquisition system between test campaigns.

The following aspects of the setup are covered in this chapter:

\begin{itemize}
\item Design of the mechanical structure for PCB, magnet, and motor integration
\item Design of the mechanical structure for resolver integration
\item Implementation of the data acquisition software
\item Development of the testing procedures
\end{itemize}

\section{System Requirements and Constraints}
\label{sec:system-requirements}

Before describing the individual components of the setup, it is important to establish what was known beforehand and what the realistic goals were. This requires answering two questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item What is needed to functionally integrate all the components of the system?
\item What constraints are imposed by the irradiation facilities?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{subsec:evaluation-methodology}

In order to develop the test procedures, it is first necessary to define the type of control that will be evaluated. The motor controller is evaluated in position control mode, for two main reasons. First, robotic arm applications are inherently servo applications, so position accuracy is the most relevant performance metric. Second, position readout from the resolver is more straightforward than velocity estimation: sampling the angle at regular intervals avoids the information loss introduced by numerical differentiation between samples, making it a more reliable metric.

The system integrates four main components: i) the motor driver PCB, ii) a 6\,mm round permanent magnet, iii) a brushless DC motor, and iv) a rotary resolver. The motor selected for this setup is the \textit{GM4108H-120T Gimbal Motor}, which was already available in large stock at CERN. This is a hollow-shaft motor, and this characteristic has direct implications on the mechanical design, as all components must be aligned along a common vertical z-axis.

\subsection{Resolver and Readout System}
\label{subsec:resolver-readout}

The resolver integrated in this setup is the same model used in the collimator stepper motor systems of the accelerators, where it is actively maintained and operated by the BE-CEM-MRO section. This choice ensures compatibility with existing infrastructure and validated readout electronics.

For reading the resolver output, the \textit{EVAL-AS2S1210} evaluation board by Analog Devices is used, which is based on the same IC used by BE-CEM-MRO in their collimator systems. The resolver requires its signal cables to be routed as twisted pairs to maintain signal integrity, which has direct consequences on the cabling strategy described in Section~\ref{subsec:resolver-cabling}.

\subsection{Irradiation Facility Constraints}
\label{subsec:facility-constraints}

The setup must be compatible with three irradiation facilities: i) CHARM, ii) PSI PIF, and iii) CC60. While these facilities differ in beam type and geometry, the most relevant parameters for setup design are the radiation environment and the available cabling infrastructure.

In CC60 and PSI PIF, only a localised region around the device under test is irradiated, so sensitive supporting equipment can be placed at a safe distance. In CHARM, however, the entire room is irradiated. This means that equipment such as UART-to-USB converters and the resolver readout board must be located outside the irradiation room in all cases, to ensure compatibility across all three facilities.

All three facilities share a similar cabling infrastructure, with patch panels supporting BNC, RJ45, and USB connectors. For this setup, BNC and RJ45 connectors are used. The RJ45 connection is particularly well suited for the resolver signal routing: since Ethernet cables internally contain twisted pairs, they can directly serve the resolver's signal integrity requirements without any additional specialised cabling. Standard Ethernet cables are used on both the irradiation room and control room sides. The resulting connectivity is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:irradiation-control-rooms}.

\section{Mechanical Design of the Test Bench}
\label{sec:cad-test-bench-design}

The mechanical structure integrating all system components was designed in SOLIDWORKS. The design is governed by strict alignment requirements, since the accuracy of the system depends on the correct positioning of each component along the common z-axis defined in the previous section:

\begin{itemize}
\item The permanent magnet must be fixed at the center of the rotor and aligned to the magnetic encoder with a maximum axial offset of 1\,mm.
\item The resolver rotor must be co-axial with the motor rotor and free to spin without any contact with the resolver stator.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Material Selection and Manufacturing}
\label{subsec:manufacturing}

The primary manufacturing method used was FDM 3D printing in ABS. ABS was chosen for its thermal resistance, which provides robustness in the event of motor overheating during fault conditions. More practically, 3D printing was the natural choice given that the structure had to be reproduced multiple times: after each CHARM test campaign, the assembled components become radioactive and cannot be handled or reused outside the irradiation room. Disposable, quickly reproduced 3D-printed structures were therefore the most practical solution.

A version of the structure was also manufactured internally at CERN in aluminium via CNC machining. Despite the tighter dimensional tolerances achievable with CNC compared to the Bambu Lab 3D printer, the resolver rotor still exhibited slight off-axis rotation, which suggests that the hollow motor rotor itself may have some inherent angular runout rather than a manufacturing deficiency in the structure.

Aluminium was selected as the preferred structural material for two reasons beyond machinability. First, it is non-magnetic, which is critical: a ferromagnetic material in close proximity would distort the field produced by the small permanent magnet and degrade encoder accuracy. Second, the aluminium structure simplified grounding considerably — it was bonded directly to the board ground at a single point, achieving a clean chassis ground and avoiding ground loops.

\subsection{Resolver Cabling}
\label{subsec:resolver-cabling}

As introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:resolver-readout}, the resolver signals must be routed as twisted pairs. Since dedicated twisted pair cables were not available in the facility, two alternatives were considered:

\begin{itemize}
\item Six individual 50\,$\Omega$ coaxial cables, with signal on the center conductor and the shield as ground reference, or
\item Standard RJ45 Ethernet cables, which contain twisted pairs internally by design.
\end{itemize}

The RJ45 solution was selected. As already motivated in Section~\ref{subsec:facility-constraints}, Ethernet cables and RJ45 patch panels are part of the standard infrastructure at all three facilities, making this choice naturally consistent with the broader connectivity strategy. Beyond signal integrity, this approach is easy to reproduce, straightforward to standardise, and significantly simpler to install than running individual coaxial cables through the facility patch panels.

Therefore, a Telegärtner MFP8-IE Cat.6\textsubscript{A} T568B RJ45 connector~\cite{telegaertner_mfp8_ie_t568b} was mounted at the resolver cable's end.

\section{Data Acquisition and Analysis Methodology}
\label{sec:data-acquisition-methodology}

\subsection{Elapsed Time and Dose Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:dose-reconstruction}

A common data processing procedure was applied to the logged quantities before analyzing the irradiation results. All acquisition systems used Unix timestamps in UTC. Therefore, the absolute timestamp of each sample was first converted to elapsed irradiation time. If $t_i$ is the timestamp of sample $i$ and $t_0$ is the timestamp corresponding to the start of irradiation, the elapsed time is

\begin{equation}
\Delta t_i = \frac{t_i - t_0}{3600},
\end{equation}

where $\Delta t_i$ is expressed in hours. Since the dose rate was calibrated at $\dot{D} = 30~\si{\gray\per\hour}$, the corresponding total ionizing dose was calculated as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TID}_i = \dot{D}\Delta t_i.
\end{equation}

This allows the measurements to be plotted directly as a function of accumulated dose, while a secondary axis can be used to show the equivalent elapsed irradiation time.

\subsection{Angular Position and Velocity Estimation}
\label{subsec:angle-velocity}

The angular position was monitored using both the resolver and the AS5047P magnetic encoder. The AS5047P provides a 14-bit raw angle value, which was converted to degrees as

\begin{equation}
\theta_i = \frac{360^\circ}{2^{14}} r_i,
\end{equation}

where $r_i$ is the raw encoder reading. Since angular measurements are periodic, the angle was unwrapped before calculating derivatives. This avoids artificial jumps when the measured angle crosses the $0^\circ/360^\circ$ boundary.

The angular velocity was then estimated from the unwrapped angle $\phi_i$ using finite differences,

\begin{equation}
\omega_i \approx \frac{\phi_{i+1} - \phi_{i-1}}{t_{i+1} - t_{i-1}},
\end{equation}

and the absolute angular speed was computed as

\begin{equation}
s_i = |\omega_i|.
\end{equation}

The absolute speed was used because the goal was to determine whether the rotor was moving or settled, independently of the rotation direction.

\subsection{Motion Segmentation}
\label{subsec:motion-segmentation}

Because differentiation amplifies measurement noise, the speed signal was smoothed before identifying the motion intervals. A rolling median filter was used, since it is less sensitive to isolated spikes than a moving average:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{s}_i = \operatorname{median}\left(s_{i-k}, \ldots, s_i, \ldots, s_{i+k}\right).
\end{equation}

In this analysis, the window length corresponds to approximately \SI{3}{\second}. The smoothing was applied only to the derived speed signal used for motion detection; the original measured angle was kept for the position plots.

The moving and settled periods were then identified automatically using two thresholds. A higher threshold was used to detect the central part of each movement, where the rotor was certainly moving. A lower threshold was then used to extend each detected interval backwards and forwards in time, so that the start and end of the motion were better aligned with the physical movement.

The thresholds were estimated from the noise floor of the smoothed speed signal using the median and the median absolute deviation:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{robust}} = 1.4826\, \operatorname{median}\left(\left|\tilde{s}_i - \operatorname{median}(\tilde{s})\right|\right).
\end{equation}

The higher threshold was used to identify the motion core, while the lower threshold was used to include the slower acceleration and deceleration regions. Very short detections were rejected, and neighbouring intervals separated by very small gaps were merged. The result is a set of motion intervals,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_j = \left[t_{\mathrm{start},j}, t_{\mathrm{end},j}\right],
\end{equation}

where the rotor is considered to be moving. Samples outside these intervals are considered settled. This procedure provides an objective way to separate the data into moving and settled states, without relying on visual inspection, and allows the same analysis to be applied consistently across all irradiation tests.

\subsection{Accuracy and Repeatability Metrics}
\label{subsec:accuracy-repeatability-metrics}

Accuracy and repeatability are two core evaluation parameters when benchmarking a control system. While they might seem similar, they correspond to different properties of the system.

\textbf{Accuracy} is the ability of the system to reach a final position close to the commanded position,

\begin{equation*}
e = |\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}} - \theta_{\mathrm{actual}}| < \delta, \qquad \delta \text{: the maximum allowable position error.}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Repeatability} is the ability of the system to reach the same actual position consistently under repeated identical commands,

\begin{equation*}
r = \max_i(\theta_i) - \min_i(\theta_i) < \rho, \qquad \rho \text{: the maximum allowable variation between repeated attempts.}
\end{equation*}

A visual reference for both metrics is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-repeatability}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{05_SystemLevelTestBench/images/Accuracy_Repeatability.pdf}
\caption{Illustration of accuracy and repeatability and their distinction for a positioning system.}
\label{fig:accuracy-repeatability}
\end{figure}

Both the encoder angle (from the telemetry UART, used directly by the control algorithm) and the independent resolver angle are used to evaluate these metrics. Before any comparison, the constant zero-reference offset between the two sensors must be isolated, since the encoder and resolver do not share the same zero angle. This offset is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:encoder-resolver-offset}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{05_SystemLevelTestBench/images/Encoder_Resolver_Offset.pdf}
\caption{The constant angular offset between encoder and resolver readings, arising from their independent zero reference calibration.}
\label{fig:encoder-resolver-offset}
\end{figure}

The encoder angle represents the accuracy of the closed-loop controller, since it is the quantity the firmware attempts to drive to the commanded value. The resolver provides an independent ground truth to validate those results. The commanded sequence used across all tests is $[0, 90, 180, 270]^\circ$.

\subsubsection*{Command Sequence and Angular Quantisation}

The demo drives the rotor to four absolute target angles, issued in firmware as integer milliradians,

\begin{equation}
c_k \in \{0,\,1571,\,3141,\,4712\}~\si{\milli\radian}, \qquad k = 0,1,2,3.
\end{equation}

Because the targets are absolute rather than incremental, the positioning error does not accumulate from cycle to cycle. Converting to degrees,

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}^{(k)} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\,\frac{c_k}{1000},
\end{equation}

gives a command resolution of $1~\si{\milli\radian} = 0.0573^\circ$. The realised targets are therefore $0^\circ$, $90.012^\circ$, $179.966^\circ$ and $269.978^\circ$, deviating from the nominal $[0,90,180,270]^\circ$ by at most $0.034^\circ$. This quantisation is a fixed, dose-independent contribution, roughly an order of magnitude below the measured positioning error, and is removed from the analysis by referencing every error to the realised $\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}^{(k)}$ rather than to the nominal angle.

\subsubsection*{Offset Removal}

The encoder and the resolver do not share a zero reference, so a constant offset must be isolated before any comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:encoder-resolver-offset}). Working modulo a full turn, the smallest signed difference between two angles is written with the wrapping operator

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{wrap}(x) = \big[(x + 180^\circ)\bmod 360^\circ\big] - 180^\circ \;\in\; (-180^\circ,\,180^\circ].
\end{equation}

Each encoder sample is matched to the temporally nearest resolver sample (within a tolerance of \SI{0.5}{\second}), yielding pairs $(\theta^{\mathrm{enc}}_i,\theta^{\mathrm{res}}_i)$. The constant offset is taken as the median of their signed difference, which is robust to the occasional transient mismatch,

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta_0 = \operatorname*{median}_i \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(\theta^{\mathrm{res}}_i - \theta^{\mathrm{enc}}_i\big).
\end{equation}

The dispersion of the same quantity, expressed as the median absolute deviation,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAD} = \operatorname*{median}_i \big|\operatorname{wrap}(\theta^{\mathrm{res}}_i-\theta^{\mathrm{enc}}_i) - \Delta\theta_0\big|,
\end{equation}

serves as a direct test of whether the offset is truly constant: a small, dose-invariant MAD confirms that the two zero references are simply shifted and that neither sensor decalibrates under irradiation.

To express the accuracy with respect to the firmware command, each sensor is referenced to the command frame by a single constant zero, calibrated only on settled samples acquired before significant dose has accumulated (low-dose baseline, $\mathrm{TID} < \SI{60}{\gray}$),

\begin{align}
\beta_{\mathrm{enc}} &= \operatorname*{median} \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}} - \theta^{\mathrm{enc}}\big), & \beta_{\mathrm{res}} &= \operatorname*{median} \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}} - \theta^{\mathrm{res}}\big).
\end{align}

\subsubsection*{Positioning Error Decomposition}

Using the motion segmentation of Section~\ref{subsec:motion-segmentation}, the analysis is restricted to the settled intervals $\mathcal{S}_j$. Each settled interval is reduced to a single circular-mean angle,

\begin{equation}
\bar{\theta}_j = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_j}\sin\theta_i,\; \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_j}\cos\theta_i\right),
\end{equation}

computed separately for the resolver and the encoder. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}(j)$ be the target associated with interval $j$. The positioning error is then decomposed into three terms, all in the command frame,

\begin{align}
e_j^{\mathrm{true}} &= \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(\bar{\theta}^{\mathrm{res}}_j + \beta_{\mathrm{res}} - \theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}(j)\big), \label{eq:e-true}\\[2pt]
e_j^{\mathrm{ctrl}} &= \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(\bar{\theta}^{\mathrm{enc}}_j + \beta_{\mathrm{enc}} - \theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}(j)\big), \label{eq:e-ctrl}\\[2pt]
e_j^{\mathrm{sens}} &= e_j^{\mathrm{true}} - e_j^{\mathrm{ctrl}}. \label{eq:e-sens}
\end{align}

Here $e^{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ is the controller error: the loop driving the encoder to its target, evaluated against the very sensor it closes on. It is necessary but not sufficient, because it is blind to any error of that sensor. $e^{\mathrm{true}}$ is the true positioning error, measured by the independent resolver; it is the trustworthy figure and the only one capable of revealing a radiation-induced sensor error. Their difference $e^{\mathrm{sens}}$ isolates the encoder's own error. The accuracy is summarised per setpoint by the mean bias and the mean absolute, RMS, $95^{\text{th}}$-percentile and maximum of $|e^{\mathrm{true}}|$.

As an offset-free check that requires no zero reference, the spacing of the settled resolver levels $L_k$ is compared with the commanded $90^\circ$ step,

\begin{equation}
\Delta_k = \operatorname{wrap}\!\big(L_{k+1} - L_k\big), \qquad \textstyle\sum_k \Delta_k = 360^\circ,
\end{equation}

and any dose dependence is quantified by the slope of $e^{\mathrm{true}}$ versus TID (ordinary least squares, with a $95\%$ confidence interval) together with the difference between the low- and high-dose means.
